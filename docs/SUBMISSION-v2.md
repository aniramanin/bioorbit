## Summary (≈1,250 characters)

BioOrbit is an offline-first health monitoring and decision-support app for astronauts on long-duration missions. Astronauts log vital signs, sleep, mood, symptoms and cabin CO₂, measure heart rate with a phone camera, take a 3-minute reaction test that screens for fatigue and a 1-minute Amsler grid vision check for SANS. BioOrbit turns these data into a colour-coded body map, a readiness score and clear actions. It compares every value with the astronaut's own baseline to catch problems early and combines signals into insights such as fatigue, possible infection or CO₂-related headache. Escalation advice adapts to the communication delay of each environment, from the ISS to Mars. It also uses real data: live solar-radiation storm status from NOAA's GOES satellites, and the blood tests of the four Inspiration4 astronauts from NASA's Open Science Data Repository. On that real data, BioOrbit's personal-baseline rule catches lymphocyte drops of 27–48 % after landing that a normal-range check calls "normal". Because crews on Mars cannot rely on real-time help from Earth, BioOrbit gives them the knowledge and autonomy to act on their own health. The same tool serves remote communities on Earth.

## Project Details

WHAT IT DOES
BioOrbit is a "health bridge" for each crew member. One look answers: How am I? What changed? What do I do now?

• Readiness score and duty status (Mission ready, Ready with caution, Limited duty, Medical attention) from 15 indicators, symptoms and baseline changes.
• Interactive body map: eight body systems, each colour-coded and clickable, explaining its function, what spaceflight does to it, the latest values and what to do now.
• Guided logging: fields turn green, amber or red as you type, and explain why each indicator matters in space and how to measure it with onboard equipment.
• Measure with what is on board, no extra hardware:
  – Camera heart rate (photoplethysmography): a fingertip over the phone camera.
  – Psychomotor Vigilance Test (PVT-B): the brief reaction test used to detect fatigue before critical operations.
  – Amsler grid vision check (NEW): per-eye self-check for Spaceflight Associated Neuro-ocular Syndrome (SANS); any distortion lights up the eyes on the body map and triggers SANS follow-up.
• Personal-baseline drift detection: flags meaningful change even when values are still "normal".
• Multi-signal insights: sleep debt + slower reactions → fatigue; fever + elevated heart rate/rash → possible infection; persistent low mood → behavioural health; high cabin CO₂ + headache → CO₂-related headache (NEW); vision change → possible SANS.
• Cabin CO₂ (NEW): habitat indicator with NASA-STD-3001-based limits. On the ISS, headache odds roughly doubled per 1 mmHg of CO₂ (Law et al., 2014, NASA LSAH data).
• Mission environments: Space Station, Lunar Base, Mars Transit, Mars Surface and Earth Remote Care, each with its own radiation dose rate (cumulative dose vs. the 600 mSv career limit), gravity, communication delay and escalation rules.
• Delay-tolerant downlink to the flight surgeon: a structured TXT/JSON report with findings, actions and 7-day trends, showing the real arrival time at Earth.
• Crew overview for the medical officer, mission routine with gamification (XP, levels, streaks, badges) and a knowledge library on NASA's five hazards.

REAL DATA, NOT JUST SIMULATIONS (NEW)
The "NASA data" page uses two real sources:
1. Live space weather: NOAA SWPC GOES ≥10 MeV proton flux, shown on the NOAA solar radiation storm scale (S0–S5) with a 24-hour chart and environment-specific advice (postpone EVAs, move to the storm shelter beyond low Earth orbit). The last reading is cached, so it still works offline.
2. Real astronaut blood tests: the complete blood count (NASA OSDR OSD-569) and metabolic panel (OSD-575) of the four SpaceX Inspiration4 crew members, 17 analytes at 7 time points before and after flight. BioOrbit applies its personal-baseline rule to this data: for lymphocytes, 3 of 4 crew members dropped 27–48 % from their own pre-flight baseline the day after landing while staying inside the Earth reference range. A population-range check would call them normal; a personal baseline does not. This is the core idea of BioOrbit, shown on real astronaut data.

WHAT IS REAL AND WHAT IS SIMULATED
Real: the NOAA proton flux, the Inspiration4 lab values, the published limits and references, and anything you measure yourself (camera pulse, reaction test, vision check, manual entries). Simulated: the 30-day mission history of the four demo crew members, generated by deterministic code so the app can be explored immediately. BioOrbit is a prototype for decision support, not a medical device.

HOW IT WORKS
Values are classified against reference ranges. A personal-baseline model compares each person's last three days with their own previous days (z-score plus a clinically meaningful minimum change), and rule-based patterns combine several signals. The camera pulse algorithm averages the red channel of the fingertip image, resamples it to 30 Hz, removes the slow trend and estimates heart rate by autocorrelation with protection against octave errors (within ±3 bpm from 42 to 178 bpm on synthetic test signals). The PVT follows the PVT-B protocol (1–4 s intervals, lapses > 355 ms, false starts < 100 ms).

BENEFITS AND IMPACT
It helps astronauts who are not doctors understand and act on their own health far from Earth, detects early change instead of waiting for a critical value, works fully offline on any phone or laptop, and keeps data private on the device. On Earth it fits remote care: isolated and riverside communities, Antarctic stations, ships and home monitoring. Next steps: wearable/Bluetooth sensors, ultrasound guidance, machine-learning baselines trained on analog-mission data and validation with health professionals.

TOOLS
HTML5, CSS3 and vanilla JavaScript with no frameworks or external libraries (so it works offline); Canvas and SVG for charts and the body map; MediaDevices/getUserMedia for the camera; localStorage for data; NOAA SWPC JSON feed; NASA OSDR data files; hosted on GitHub Pages.
